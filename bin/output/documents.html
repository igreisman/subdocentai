effect renewal of GROWLER'S fathometer head, many discussions were
held. Enclosures (D) to (G) inc. of the Task Group Commander's
Supplementary Patrol instructions were promulgated. The three of
the important areas of the Rotating Patrol were blocked out in
squares 20 miles on a side. The squares in each area were given
horizontal and vertical coordinates in grid form - See Enclosure
(B).

17 August 1944 and operated in accordance with references (A) and
(B). Communication drills were held and some changes to the plan
were made. Only the 2,000 Kc band will be used for communication
within this Group. The VHF was found to be effective to 7,500
yards maximum. Recognition by radar interference keying is supple-
mented by individual ship identification within this Group. Com-
munication by radar may be resorted to during coordinated attacks.
It is necessary for each unit to have four high frequency receivers
in order to guard HAIKU, CHINA, AREA FREQUENCY and the Group
frequency.

was light and two-engine speed would put us in our individual
areas on the twenty-ninth in accordance with serial 37. Interval

Subject: British and Australian Prisoner-of-War Survivors - Rescue of.

(A) NARRATIVE

On the afternoon of 15 September 1944, while patrolling on the surface on an easterly heading from HAINAN ISLAND in the South China Sea, at Latitude 18° 42' N., Longitude 114° 00' E., the O.O.D. and bridge lookouts sighted a lifeboat and a large quantity of debris floating on the surface of a calm sea. This spot was a little to the north of where an enemy convoy had been intercepted and attacked by our submarines on the night of 11-12 September 1944. There was a possibility of the lifeboat being occupied so course was altered to go alongside, only to find it abandoned. At this time, 1610(I), a lookout reported that there were two rafts on the horizon with men on them. It was naturally believed that these would be Japanese, so small arms were broken out and preparations made for taking prisoners.

On the approach to the first raft, which was a makeshift affair of hatch covers and timbers, it was noted that the fifteen men it contained were thickly covered with crude-oil and were clothed very scantily, though some had nondescript hats and a few wore the regulation Japanese soldier's caps. They were waving frantically and all shouting at will so that no words could be distinguished, although one voice was finally heard to speak distinctly in English. While getting the raft alongside, orders were given to bring aboard the English speaking man, and by the time the first line was over the amazing announcement came to the bridge, "They are British and Australian Prisoner-of-War and Japs survivors.

All hands went into action immediately; those below decks sent up bales of rags and began preparing hot soup and tea in quantities; the pharmacist's mate assembled his gear for immediate treatment, and the rescue party commenced bringing the survivors aboard. The poor fellows were so frantic with joy at this unbelievable piece of good fortune that they began jumping from the raft and trying to clamber up the sides of the ship. Members of the rescue party dived over the side to make the raft fast, herded the survivors back onto the raft and aided them up to the ship's deck. The weakened condition of these men, as well as their covering of slippery crude-oil, made the actual recovery quite difficult. Some of them could help themselves, but the majority had to be lifted and dragged aboard bodily.

The next problem was to clean some of the oil from their bodies and to treat their immediate needs before sending them below. While this job was being done the second raft was reached and the true bearings taken of three others within sight. The second raft contained nine men who fortunately were comparatively free from oil, making the problem of bringing them aboard much easier. While heading for the next group, a small raft was passed close aboard with its single occupant dead with part of the head gone (Probably by a shark). Recovered third and fourth raft-loads of eleven emaciated ex-prisoners-of-war. Another small raft was sighted with one figure lying motionless on his back. He seemed to be still alive and when one of the ship's crew dived into the water with a line, he sat up, startled and started to go over the side. The man in the water persuaded him to remain seated until he could be helped aboard. This man was almost blind and in a semi-conscious condition when brought aboard and remained unconscious almost continuously until he died the following evening.

(A) NARRATIVE (Continued)

While the eleven men from the next raft were being brought aboard the after lookout reported "Three planes in formation astern". There were approximately fifty men topside at this time including bridge personnel and the rescue squad as well as many survivors who had as yet not been lowered down the hatch. Fortunately one of the planes was seen to flap its wings proving the formation to be large birds gliding in perfect order.

This completed the rescue of all survivors in the immediate vicinity, but a bearing had been taken on a raft now out of sight to the south, so the ship was headed in that direction at high speed. At 1810, two rafts were sighted ahead and soon thirteen more survivors were being rescued. While getting the men from the last of these two rafts the high periscope watch sighted another raft at 020° T. and also what appeared to be a ship's masts bearing 037° T. at the extreme range of vision. While unable to verify this report of the raft, the ship was identified as a submarine that appeared to have a gun aft, making us very suspicious, since one of the survivors had stated that a German submarine was somewhere in this area (later proved to be SEALION with men on deck aft) and since it did not look or signal like an American, we dived to periscope depth and after identifying and watching the submarine for a few minutes through the periscope and noting that it was practically dead in the water, decided that it must be the SEALION rescuing survivors. We since we had sent a message to her earlier requesting that she come over and assist in rescue. So surfaced and resumed searching. It was almost completely dark by this time. A single man on a small raft was seen waving what appeared to be a white flag, but he was at some time, but later proved to be his "bleached hand". He was recovered and course was set for the last raft that had been sighted before diving. This raft was luckily located in the dark and another twelve men were rescued and stowed below making a total of seven-three (73) survivors on board.

Once below decks the main problem was the examination to determine the injuries and sicknesses. In this respect, DEEDS, M.L., PhM1c, USN, showed tireless energy and outstanding professional knowledge of the highest order. The survivors most acute need was water, which they were given in small quantities through the rags followed by hot tea, soup or broth for those who were conscious. As all these men were suffering from acute exhaustion, exposure, shock, starvation, dehydration and skin sores, their needs were many, but since the ship had necessarily to retain its military efficiency, and since it was not known what contagious diseases some of them might have, it was decided to place all but six of the most critical cases in the after torpedo room (the six were put on bunks in one corner of the after battery compartment) where, even though it was more difficult to tend their needs, they could be isolated until given a more thorough examination. One head was set aside for the use of the survivors; they were given separate eating utensils which were washed and stowed apart from those of the crew, and two officers and several crew members were assigned the nursing detail. The entire crew showed an exemplary attitude and volunteered cheerfully to do all tasks and make any sacrifices that would help the survivors in any way.

(B) TREATMENT OF SURVIVORS WHILE ON BOARD

The general condition of the survivors when rescued was critical, with every man suffering from acute exhaustion, exposure, shock, starvation, dehydration and salt sores. About seventy percent of these men had drunk sea water, urine or blood during their four days on the rafts, and nearly all were covered with fuel oil.

As the survivors were brought aboard they were treated on deck as follows: the crude-oil was wiped and then sponged off with diesel oil, and morphine was administered to those suffering from extreme shock (15 syrettes being used in this time). As soon as emergency cleaning and shock treatment were given, the men were lowered through the after battery hatch, given a cup of hot tea, a sip of not vegetable soup, after which each was given an eye irrigation followed by Butyn Sulfate and Boric Acid Ointment was applied to ease the irritation on the skin of each man's scrotum. This irritation being caused by the crude-oil.

Six of the more critical cases, who were given bunks in the after battery compartment, had their sores and ulcerations cleaned with Azochloramide in Triscetin 1:500 and dressed with Sulfathiazole Ointment 5%.

About one quarter of the survivors were given sedatives of Phenobarbital or Sodium Amytal, depending upon each one's condition. Three men had rather severe cases of malaria, one of whom was in a state of delirium for four hours after being rescued. These men were given Quinine Hydrochloride grs. XX followed by grs. X every four hours. Two men found to have severe Cellutitis infections were placed in bunks with parts elevated and dosed with Sulfadiazine grs. XV with Sodium Bicarbonate at four hour intervals rather than the prescribed dose of 4 gm. of Sulfa, because of their condition. We were taken to transit camps in our own battalion in readiness to go up to the front lines to fight that savage and inhuman enemy the devil's of the East (Japs). My battalion went up to the front lines on the 12th of Feb. and it was then we knew what hell was. Fifth column was dead against us and we had no air support at all. Friday, the 13th of February, 1942, was a day never to be forgotten by any of us. Bombs rained on us from everywhere, and men were being slaughtered left and right. Sunday, the 15th of February 1942, at 1600 a BR came along and gave order of unconditional surrender.

We were now P.O.Ws. of the Japs. Hunger had taken us and it was a very downhearted army that marched 16 miles to the Changi Barracks on the 17th.

From then on, up to October 14, 1944, we were worked on buildings and salvage dumps, and the guards over us were like slave drivers with a whip in their hand. Our pay per day, by the time we had finished a day's work we had earned $25.00. Our food consisted of broken rice and a very small ration of fish and seaweed. On 14th May, we were taken to Thailand to build a railroad from Bankok,to Moeean in Burma. We travelled through Malaya in railway trucks with 32 men in each. We marched 150 miles through Thailand, through uninhabited jungles and rice fields - up to our knees in mud and slime. The only mean beings we saw were the wild hillmen of Siam, in their own kampongs with a score or so of water buffalo wallowing in the mud. Men were falling in their tracks and the guards were beating them with split lengths of bamboo. We arrived at our first jungle home on 21st and we had to sleep in mosquito-infested swamps. This place was called Wampo and it was here we found what living hell was. We lost 27 men within a month and things were coming very hard for us. Food consisted of plain rice and salt water for well over the first month. Men were being ill-treated by the Japs in every shape and form, working from dawn to dusk clearing virgin jungle and rock for this thought-impossible, mad idea. Men of other jungle working battalions were marching through our camp every day to settle in other jungle camps, made of bamboo and atop huts, further up the river. The British Officer in charge of our camp was a man who will never be forgotten by those who knew him. He was known as the "Grand Old Man of the River", Colonel Lilley of the 1/5 Forresters, and it was through his stubborness that the Japs made things slightly easier for us. Disease was hitting all camps by now, and the river which was called Leie Laie, was soon known as the "River of Death". The first Christmas arrived and the Japs opened their hearts and gave us two old cows for 1500 men.

In January, 1943, the word came through that the railroad had to be finished within one year, and so the work must be speeded up. As the work sped up, so did the death rate, and the beatings-up by these small savages. We had a little extra food, consisting of fresh vegetables and buffalo meat, but the things we wanted were medical supplies which were absolutely refused. A. Story of BARNES, H.D., #271190, Private, 4th Royal Norfolk Regiment, British Army - On 29 October 1941, the 18th Division left Liverpool, England for convoy to the East. I was one of the guys in an infantry battalion in the 54th Brigade, 18th Division, British Army.

Left Bombay, India, January 18th, 1942 for Singapore. Arrived Singapore January 27, 1942. Japanese troops had already taken most of Malaya, we were put in first line of defense on February 9, 1942.

There was nothing much to do until the Nip troops landed on Singapore the night of February 9, 1942. We were all ready for them. All through the night heavy guns pounded the coast, more fell in our yards short of our positions. At dawn, they halted. It was reported thousands of Japanese troops had been killed, but they had landed enough coast on the west side to get their mortars into action against our defense lines. We were under mortar fire for a two-hour, continuous barrage. Ahead of our lines, green gun carriers were maneuvering for positions to attack these mortars; but had to return owing to direct machine-gunning and bombing of the roads and areas around.

My platoon (mortar) was ordered into action at 7 o'clock. The first barrage silenced a nest of Jap mortars almost at once, so we waited for awhile. Suddenly our section patrols appeared with several men short, telling us a battalion of Japs were only a few hundred yards from our positions. We had orders to pick up and withdraw, but our section commander asked for one mortar to stay in position. We volunteered. I was given a tommy gun with six others, one each, who ordered to go to forward positions to cover mortar if they had to withdraw. Suddenly there was a crash behind us; we threw ourselves down. Looking back to where it came from we saw there was nothing left of the mortar or the two operators. They were the first casualties we had; but not the last, for out of the sky they came, mortar shell upon mortar shell, crashing and smashing down upon the platoon withdrawing. Every mortar of the platoon was smashed, parts lying all around along the road side; and our mortar shells were exploding; deadly shrapnel flying amongst us. 18 were killed and 10 wounded. It was a hopeless scene. Me and the section commander were the only six left, owing to the fact we were down flat.

We waited there until the Jap mortars halted their firing, then ran for the deep drains by the roadside. The Japs must have used at least 20 mortars. On the way up to our lines, was the start of the hell on earth. We had nothing but tommy guns left, only 4 of them between us; but we had to stay in our positions with not a hope of getting a chance to get the Jap mortars and save the rifle companys from the deadly murder in their lines.

(V) REMARKS

1. Interpack communications were extremely satisfactory, although the changing character of the patrol and the impossibility of doing much advance planning before departure made an excessive amount of transmissions necessary. Since all boats had ECM, that channel was used almost exclusively in promulgating patrol plans and area assignments,— the use of the ROMEO Code for these purposes is extremely awkward and unsatisfactory. Frequent conferences were held by VHF and were conducted with a full realization of the insecurity of this channel.

2. Communications with PHILIPPINES-based aircraft were very unsatisfactory at first but improved rapidly during the patrol. In several cases we failed to get reports of downed aviators until 12-24 hours after the ditching,— the intelligence having come via PHILIPPINES-COMSUPAC-NPM FOX, whereas we had a submarine on lifeguard duty nearby as well as one or more others usually also guarding that frequency with spare receivers. We cannot escape the conviction that had the planes communicated directly with us at the time and given us full and complete information at least some of these searches would have been successful. Happily this situation was well on the way to being cleared up before we left the area.

3. Communications with aircraft could have been immensely improved if we had access to their frequency plans and calls used. We were given calls for specific strikes, but friendly planes were with us almost constantly in addition to these strikes. PIRANHA accidentally intercepted transmissions from and established contact with search planes on 2274 kc. and this proved to be a useful channel. The lifeguard frequency at first was so cluttered with routine administrative and operational traffic that it was essentially useless for its primary purpose, but this situation too was showing rapid improvement.

4. Four definite and other possible encounters with enemy submarines or torpedo-firing small craft emphasizes the enhanced danger from that source as our aviators are driving the surface anti-submarine craft out of the "outer" areas.

5. The almost universal tendency of our night flying aircraft to come directly in on a b earing run made the patrol almost as unpleasant as the JAPS could have, and furthermore its purpose is not understood. Presumably they will not attack an unidentified target in a submarine patrol zone, so apparently they make this approach to see if the contact will dive. But usually, even if the contact did not dive! On the whole there is a crying need for a clean-cut and relatively enforced operating policy for these "shared" areas that will relieve the submarine of the rather gruelling necessity of treating our extensive night air search as enemy.
