Subject: U.S.S. PILOTFISH - Report of Sixth War Patrol.

(A) PROLOGUE

Arrived Guam Bay, F.I. from Fifth War Patrol on 12 February 1945. As we were the first submarine to be refitted in Guam, recreational facilities were very limited, but the refit was accomplished effectively by the U.S.S. GRIFFIN and Submarine Division 122. Detached Ensign EDWARD DEY on F.I.M. on 22 February 1945. Received one day of training and two days loading and were ready for sea on 25 February 1945.

(C) WEATHER

The northeast monsoon was steady in the mouth of the Gulf of Siam, south of Cochin China. Both wind and sea were from the northeast, with wind varying from about force two to force four. In general, the weather conditions followed closely those as described in coast pilot, with frequent overcast skies, some haziness, and light rain squalls.

From open to Saipan, encountered heavy weather almost daily, with heavy head seas. Skies were usually overcast.

(D) TIDAL INFORMATION

In the inner Gulf of Siam the set was to the southwest averaging about 0.9 knots. It was steady during March. Over near the Malay Coast off GREAT REDANG Island the set was southward about -0.5 knots. This corresponds with current as increased to about 1-2 knots along the coast, South of GREAT REDANG group the southerly current increased to about 1-2 knots along the coast.

(G) AIRCRAFT CONTACTS

We had 30 contacts with enemy planes while in the area and were bothered through-out the patrol by the efficient use of search planes. While no boat in our wolf pack was attacked at any instant that the Japs kept track of us and routed traffic where we were.

In all one instance all we have evidence of airborne radar, that on 23 March 1945. At no time on the area did we encounter planes at night.

By far the majority of our plane contacts were by radar so we have little information regarding types in operation.

On 21 March 1945 in position Lat. 05° 37' N. and Long. 104° 20' E., we sighted six wrecks of a Japanese plane in the water.

(J) ANTI-SUBMARINE MEASURES AND EVASION TACTICS

Other than the SJ contact at extreme range on a possible high speed destroyer the night of 18 March 1945, the only contacts we had were with enemy planes. These occurred almost daily, both for us and for the other boats in the pack. Due to intermittent cloudy skies, very seldom were we able to have sight contact at prior to diving. At no time did any of these planes close us fast, nor did they attempt to drive home an attack on any of us although I am sure that we were sighted more than once. On one occasion while we were proceeding on surface at full speed during daylight to intercept a contact (as reported by one of the other submarines and which later was identified as a hospital ship) we were forced down three different times by enemy planes, which caused us to about 2 miles. Just prior to diving on one of these occasions, we sighted a Jap two-engine bomber escorted by a fighter circling us at 7-8 miles. At the time there were in all, five submarines including us running on the surface trying to intercept this ship contact and I am sure the Japs had us all under surveillance. In the meantime, it is possible that a convoy sneaked by us to the westward near the coast, because two days later a convoy was sighted west of Cape Krumus and south of Cape Jacques by one of our patrol planes in the vicinity. The intermittent cloudy skies and sometimes hazy weather make it expedient to use an intermittent sweep on SD radar during daylight hours (5 second sweep every 2-3 minutes) and it worked fine as far as detecting the aircraft, but in a positive test it was also a "Dead give-away" as to our location and that the Japs kept us pretty well spotted in both night and day by their detecting equipment.

(K) MAJOR DEFECTS AND DAMAGE

#2 and #4 Machinery - #2 air compressor motor flooded out when a plug in the main water circulating system carried away due to corrosion. Two shunt field coils and an interpole coil developed a zero ground and were replaced. #1 main engine has a cracked cylinder liner water jacket. This is the same defect which three liners were replaced during the last refit for this same defect. The crack occurred around the starting air adapter. When the starting air adapters to these cylinders were removed, it was found that they were badly pitted or corroded allowing exhaust gas to enter the water jacket probably causing the cracks.

#2 and #4 main motors again developed a badly unbalanced condition. This condition has occurred after running with 75% or more load for a period of

(L) RADIO

Two defects were experienced during this patrol. The "C" freq. band in the RBH receiver and H. F from 3.12 in one R.L Freq.

The signal from Bakers Fox faded daily between 0500 and 0700 zebra on all frequencies. A complete log of receiving conditions is being submitted to GNO as a separate report.

POLLITO cleared six messages to shore stations without too great delay; three direct to NPM and VIX6 and four via RBH. In all cases we received a receipt for them.

The best times to make were 2200, but the limits of the Wolf Pack Code were noted. POLLITO delivered one message to the U.L.M while she was anchored, but could not hear the JBF answer until she had surfaced. Each boat would have the best when diving for planes by sending a spare code group until the antenna went under. This was very successful at all times.

On 26 February 1945 we conducted several exercises with a Navy Search Plane with the idea of working out a procedure for smooth Lifo-submarine cooperation. A satisfactory system has now been worked out but we repeat here what we found in working with the plane for general information.

The biggest problem we always was the proper calibration of the two sets of equipment. When we first made rendezvous, the plane had been calling us by voice a half an hour without result, yet later we worked each other out to seventy miles and signal strength was never less than five.

Using VeryCall on the frequencies listed in Griffin able was not too satisfactory. 20 miles was about the maximum working range, but it is felt that this is not a fair indication as our voice modulation equipment is not the best and has given us trouble before.

JBF again proved unreliable at ranges over 15 miles. When the plane was overhead communications were good except when his bearing was 000° or 180° relative from us. On these bearings his signal and ours faded completely. A curious thing happened, when after diving and then surfacing, the plane noted that our signal strength on VHF for the same ranges was almost doubled. No explanation is offered for this.

(c) RADAR

SJ-1 Radar - The SJ-1 operation was normal during this patrol. On 7 March 1945 two hours operational time were lost when the "A" Scope sweep disappeared. The trouble was a shorted C-33.1 in the power supply for the cathode ray tube. The intensity of the sweep in the range indicator was increasing gradually. On 19 March 1945 a thorough check was made of the circuit but the trouble could not be found. To restore the intensity control potentiometer, P-11 (25,000 ohms.) was replaced by a spare P-8 (70,000 ohms.). (B) DENSITY LAYERS

Bathythermograph Cards were taken for each dive made on station. The shallow water in the assigned patrol area limited the deepest dive to 150 feet.

In both calm seas and zone of force five the cards with but one exception were is-thermal, the one exception being for the dive of 7 March 1945 in 37 fathoms of water and with seas and wind of force one. This card showed an is-ballast to 60 feet, a negative gradient of 1.0 from 60 feet to 80 feet and an is-ballast from 80 feet to 120 feet.

The temperature of the surface water varied from 80° to 82° F., while the temperature of the air varied from 77° to 84°.

(F) HEALTH, FOOD AND HABITABILITY

The health in general was good. There were several colds the first week after leaving Subic Bay. One arm was on the binnacle list for two weeks with a fever from cold.

The meals were well planned and served, except that 354 lbs. of meat spoiled and had to be surveyed thereby limiting the menu slightly.

Being the first boat to refit in Subic Bay, the recreational facilities there were very limited. The crew lived on board during the refit.

The conduct of the personnel on board during this run was very satisfactory and in keeping with the highest traditions of the Naval Service.

(9) REMARKS

Since the PIPERITO had spent her last patrol in this area (S-2) and had made its only two contacts on enemy convoys well to seaward of the mouth of the Gulf of Siam, and not knowing that some of the northbound traffic moved well inside the Gulf we considered it both wise and feasible for the pack to remain at sea and to put patrol line in this area to cover the most likely routes for shipping both inshore and to seaward. In order to do this thoroughly, and to co-ordinate during daylight was almost a necessity. This method has been described in the narrative of patrol with no contacts.

The PIPERITO having had only a short run from Subic to the area had ample fuel to continue her patrolling during daylight the remainder of the patrol, which has either sentries not so well-supplied, having come up from Australia, and in order to conserve our southern boundary of area so extended from 6° 00' N to 5° 00' N in order to give us a shorter and more compact area- not patrol line from the Malay Coast eastward and allow port time submerged patrolling for the other boats. This request was granted and at the same time Command was ordered to join our pack.

At this time an east-west staggered patrol line was established between Lats. 5° and 6° N. from the Malay Coast eastward for about 150 miles. The two boats inshore on this line remained submerged during daylight to avoid detection or sinkings while the two boats to seaward maintained surface cruising. It was while using this plan that the SEALION sank an unescorted tanker off the coast at night, and we were the only contact to seaward with a high speed destroyer, although we were unable to close for an attack. There were two pilots contacts made by the pack prior to our leaving the patrol area, at which time the Commanding Officer of the SEALION became ComSubPac. This will in turn be covered, the PIPERITO remained on the surface at all times, except when diving for plane contacts and for one day while effecting repairs to gyro compass, in order to maintain radio guard and to coordinate disposition of wolfpack in case of contacts.

1. The Sixth War Patrol of the U.S.S. BATFISH was conducted in the Gulf of Siam during the period 25 February - 24 April 1945. Two 17-seawar days were spent in the area, one day submerged. The Commanding Officer was also in command of a group consisting of Batfish, Caiman, Guilla and Piper.

No plane contacts were made at night. There was only one indication of airborne radar. Eight floating mines were sighted.

3. The SD radar was used by keying about 5 seconds every 2 to 3 minutes. The C.O. states that the SD was fine for detecting planes but undoubtedly kept the Japs advised of the Submarine's approximate location. The evidence is rather conclusive that this is precisely what happens when either the SD or SJ is used very much. They are detected and DF'd. Though the Japs cannot actually home, traffic will not be routed through that area and search planes will cover the submarine. The instantaneous keyed SD with persistent scope partially solves the SD problem. Possibly the Japs cannot even detect ST yet and its use as a search radar might be indicated. It does not, however, have the range of the SJ. When SD or SJ are used for search purposes the keying must be at irregular intervals, as done by the Terponite.
